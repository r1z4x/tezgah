# Report on “Detect Before You Attribute: Cascade Failure Attribution for Multi-Agent Systems”

## 1. Authors and Institutions

The paper is authored by Jiayi Zhang, Zexin Wang, Degang Sun, Changhua Pei, Gaogang Xie, and Jingjing Li of the **Computer Network Information Center, Chinese Academy of Sciences**. Fei Sun is affiliated with the **Institute of Computing Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences**.

## 2. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Large language model (LLM)-based multi-agent systems are increasingly used for tasks requiring planning, tool use, delegation, and interaction with external environments. Frameworks such as AutoGen, ChatDev, MetaGPT, and CAMEL organize multiple agents into workflows in which one agent’s output can become another agent’s input. This creates dependencies that are more complex than those in single-agent systems.

A central reliability problem in these systems is **cascading failure**. An early hallucination, incorrect tool call, or misunderstanding can propagate through subsequent steps and produce a final task failure that appears far from its original cause. Failure attribution seeks to identify both **which agent** introduced the problem and **which execution step** was responsible.

Existing failure-attribution methods generally fall into two groups. Topology- and spectrum-based methods, such as FAMAS and CDC-MAS, use execution frequencies, dependencies, or causal relationships. They are computationally efficient but may overlook semantic errors that do not visibly alter the execution structure. LLM-as-a-Judge approaches, including ECHO and CORRECT, can interpret textual content and reason about the meaning of individual steps. However, they become expensive and less reliable when processing long trajectories. The paper connects this problem to the “lost-in-the-middle” effect, in which relevant information in long contexts is more difficult for language models to use effectively.

DUOTRACE is positioned between these approaches. It does not replace an LLM judge or attempt to make the final causal decision independently. Instead, it introduces a preliminary anomaly-detection stage that identifies suspicious nodes and supplies a smaller, failure-relevant sub-trajectory to an existing attribution system. Its main research contribution is therefore a **detect-before-attribute pipeline** combining semantic representations, execution topology, hierarchical encoding, and data augmentation.

## 3. Objectives and Motivation

### 3.1 Central Objective

The primary objective is to improve failure attribution in long, hierarchical multi-agent execution traces. Specifically, the framework aims to improve:

1. **Agent-level attribution**, or identifying the agent responsible for the failure.
2. **Step-level attribution**, or locating the specific execution step where the failure originated.
3. **Inference efficiency**, measured through token consumption and execution time.
4. **Robustness**, particularly when semantic log information is incomplete or when execution structures are not strictly trees.

The authors formulate an agent trajectory as a directed hierarchical graph, usually a tree, in which each node represents an execution step. A node contains an agent identity, an invoked operator or tool, and a textual payload. Edges represent parent-child invocation relationships.

### 3.2 Motivation for Detecting Before Attributing

The paper distinguishes anomaly detection from causal attribution. DUOTRACE does not claim that every anomalous node is the root cause. Instead, it estimates which nodes deviate from patterns learned from successful executions. These nodes are ranked and passed to a downstream LLM judge, which performs the final attribution.

This separation addresses two limitations of direct attribution. First, raw trajectories may contain many irrelevant steps, increasing the likelihood that the judge overlooks the actual failure. Second, sending full logs to an LLM consumes substantial tokens and increases latency. A compact candidate trajectory can reduce both context dilution and computational expense.

The authors identify three technical challenges. Agent traces contain both semantic information and structural dependencies; their hierarchical rather than sequential organization is difficult for standard encoders; and high-quality normal trajectories are often scarce because failures are easier to collect than reliable successful examples.

## 4. Methodology and Approach

### 4.1 Overall Pipeline

DUOTRACE consists of four main stages:

1. **Data augmentation** creates additional successful trajectories.
2. **Dual-view feature construction** represents semantic and structural information.
3. **A Tree-LSTM variational autoencoder (VAE)** learns normal execution patterns and reconstructs expected node behavior.
4. **Dynamic scoring and integration** ranks suspicious nodes and forwards a reduced sub-trajectory to a downstream LLM attribution method.

The framework is described as plug-and-play because it can be placed before different LLM-based attribution systems, including direct prompting, ECHO, and CORRECT.

### 4.2 Data Augmentation

The VAE is trained primarily on normal execution behavior. To address the limited availability of successful trajectories, the paper uses two augmentation methods.

First, **LLM replay generation** asks a language model to reproduce the original task through a logically successful trajectory. The generated trace must use an allowed agent vocabulary, begin with a Human node, contain structured fields for each step, and end with a valid task result. The paper reports generating five successful replays per training task, producing more than 900 augmented trajectories. Generated outputs are checked programmatically against the task-level ground truth.

Second, **prefix-chain extraction** divides successful trajectories into variable-length prefixes. These prefixes represent valid intermediate states and increase the diversity of normal execution patterns. The authors state that synthesis is limited to the training split and does not use the diagnostic ground truth identifying the actual failure location.

### 4.3 Dual-View Representation

The semantic view uses the `all-MiniLM-L6-v2` Sentence Transformer. Agent identities and invoked operations are embedded as 384-dimensional vectors and concatenated into a 768-dimensional representation. This is then projected to 128 dimensions. Embedding role and operator descriptions rather than relying solely on categorical identifiers is intended to support transfer to semantically similar but previously unseen agents or tools.

The structural view is represented through the adjacency matrix of the execution graph. This records which node invokes which child node. Combining the semantic representation with the topology allows the detector to model cases in which an action is unusual because of its location or role in the execution tree, even if the action itself is not statistically rare.

The formal node definition also includes a textual payload. However, the implementation details emphasize agent and operator embeddings together with token-consumption prediction; the paper does not fully specify a separate direct embedding mechanism for the complete payload.

### 4.4 Tree-LSTM VAE

The encoder is a Child-Sum Tree-LSTM. For each node, it combines the projected semantic features with the hidden and cell states of its children. This allows the representation to reflect hierarchical dependencies and nested tool calls. The encoder intentionally excludes the current node’s token consumption from its input, preventing direct leakage of the quantity that the decoder is expected to predict.

The encoded hidden state parameterizes a Gaussian latent distribution with a 64-dimensional latent space. The decoder reconstructs the agent and operator embeddings and predicts token consumption as a conditional Gaussian distribution. The training objective is an evidence lower bound consisting of:

- Mean squared error for semantic reconstruction.
- Negative log-likelihood for token-consumption prediction.
- Kullback–Leibler divergence to regularize the latent distribution.

The anomaly score for a node combines semantic reconstruction error and token-prediction error. A high score indicates that the node’s role, operation, or execution footprint is inconsistent with the patterns learned from successful traces.

### 4.5 Dynamic Scoring and Candidate Selection

Static thresholds can be unreliable in long trajectories because reconstruction errors accumulate and different tasks have different normal ranges. DUOTRACE therefore standardizes node scores using a baseline mean and standard deviation derived from successful traces. The resulting anomaly Z-scores measure relative deviation.

The method also tracks early score increases, described as “mutation peaks,” because an early deviation may represent the beginning of a cascade, whereas later deviations may be downstream symptoms. The highest-scoring nodes are selected, with the default setting retaining approximately the top 20% of nodes. Their adjacent structural context is preserved before the resulting sub-trajectory is passed to the downstream judge.

## 5. Main Findings and Results

### 5.1 Attribution Accuracy

Experiments use the Who and When benchmark, containing 184 failure instances: 58 complex, long-step Handcraft cases and 126 shorter Automated cases. The evaluation measures agent accuracy and step accuracy.

The enhanced systems generally outperform their unfiltered counterparts. For example, overall performance for CORRECT increases from **62.5% to 69.1% in agent accuracy** and from **41.9% to 49.0% in step accuracy**. ECHO increases from **54.9% to 62.4%** at the agent level and from **35.9% to 40.7%** at the step level. With direct prompting, Qwen3.5-plus rises from **49.5%/31.5%** to **63.0%/38.0%** in overall agent/step accuracy, while GPT-5 rises from **44.6%/16.3%** to **59.2%/29.3%**.

The paper reports aggregate absolute improvements of **8.7 percentage points for agent-level attribution** and **7.0 points for step-level attribution** across the evaluated LLM-based baselines. Gains are often larger on the Handcraft subset, where long trajectories create greater context problems. For example, Qwen3.5-plus improves by 19.0 points in Handcraft agent accuracy, and GPT-5 improves by 15.5 points.

### 5.2 Ablation Results

The ablation studies support the proposed design choices. With the top 20% of nodes retained, the full model recalls the actual root cause at **86.21%** on Handcraft and **97.62%** on Automated. Removing prefix-chain extraction lowers Handcraft recall by 8.62 points, indicating that valid intermediate prefixes help model temporal execution progression. Removing LLM-generated successful traces lowers Automated recall by 5.56 points.

Both semantic and structural views are important. Removing the semantic view reduces Handcraft recall by 20.69 points, while removing the structural view reduces it by 18.97 points. Removing the agent, operator, or token-prediction losses also causes substantial degradation. Replacing the VAE with a deterministic autoencoder lowers recall by 6.90 points on Handcraft, supporting the use of a probabilistic latent space for modeling legitimate execution variability.

Filtering quality matters beyond context reduction alone. DUOTRACE outperforms random filtering, rule-based matching, and positional Top-K filtering across the tested backbones. On Handcraft with Qwen3.5-plus, agent accuracy is 72.4% with DUOTRACE, compared with 43.1% for random filtering, 36.2% for rule-based matching, and 10.3% for positional filtering.

### 5.3 Efficiency and Robustness

On the Handcraft data, DUOTRACE reduces token consumption by 41% for ECHO and 76% for Qwen3.5-plus. Reported inference time decreases by 60% for CORRECT and 64% for Qwen3.5-plus.

The framework also retains gains when 20% of semantic fields are masked. Across evaluated methods, average degradation is 5.9 points without DUOTRACE versus 2.6 points with it. Structural information therefore provides some resilience when text is incomplete.

Additional tests show improvements on non-tree traces after structural unrolling, on AgentErrorBench and RealWorld, and in multi-cause ranking on MP-Bench. In the latter setting, DUOTRACE improves nDCG@5, indicating better prioritization of relevant candidates rather than proof of a single causal root. Zero-shot tests on held-out task categories also show consistent improvements. Performance is most favorable when approximately 20–25% of nodes are retained; more aggressive pruning can omit the cause, while retaining too many nodes can reintroduce context noise.

## 6. Significance and Potential Impact

DUOTRACE contributes a modular approach to diagnosing failures in LLM-based multi-agent systems. Its main practical implication is that failure attribution can be treated as a two-stage problem: a relatively lightweight model first identifies deviations from normal execution, and an LLM then performs semantic and causal interpretation on a smaller evidence set. This division may make debugging more affordable for long-running agent workflows and may support online monitoring.

The method also illustrates the value of combining execution topology with language-based representations. Structural information helps track propagation and hierarchical dependencies, while semantic features help identify hallucinations, role mismatches, and tool misuse that frequency-based methods may miss.

Several limitations temper the conclusions. The principal benchmark contains only 184 cases and is dominated by information-retrieval tasks. The detector depends on successful trajectories, including synthetically generated ones, and may learn biases from the replay model or the benchmark’s execution conventions. Anomaly scores are indicators of deviation, not causal proof; downstream LLM judges remain necessary and may introduce their own biases. The current architecture is designed for trees and handles non-tree graphs through structural unrolling rather than native graph modeling. Finally, the optimal 20% retention rate is empirically selected and may vary across systems.

Overall, the paper presents DUOTRACE as a front-end filtering mechanism that improves attribution accuracy, reduces LLM inference costs, and offers a pathway toward more interpretable debugging of hierarchical multi-agent systems.
