## What it gives

The decisive evidence on the axis that decides this survey's answer. The paper relaxes
the single-process restriction of the spatiotemporal-composability calculus to an
arbitrary assignment of components and records to processes, gives four sufficient
conditions, and proves (Theorem 1, from four lemmas) that the reversibility guarantee
holds **across** processes when those conditions are met. Logos is the construction: a
peer-process, name-routed agent "in the form of ROS, where a plugin is a process, the
router holds only a rebuildable routing table, and the session state needed for recovery
lives in an append-only transcript owned by no process."

The measured contrast, over 200 benchmark tasks under one fault across three
configurations: the single-process reference **lost every session** and scored 1.5% on
the official validator; the MCP configuration kept its sessions while spending 1099 calls
on a dead endpoint; Logos kept every session alive, wasted zero calls, and succeeded on
120 tasks against 102 for both configurations combined. Mechanism level: 80 sessions
terminated at four points of the tool-call cycle all resumed with no repeated action,
3,500 concurrent calls paired with zero violations, and one bus hop cost 1/823 of the
model's first token (the authors' own figure, not reproduced here).

## What it means for tezgah

This is why the verdict on an async execution engine for tezgah is **reject** rather than
"later". The fault-tolerance guarantee is bought specifically by moving components and
records *out of the failing process*, into a router plus an append-only transcript owned
by no process — precisely the long-lived process and shared store tezgah's hook shape
does not have. "Adopt the guarantee" and "stay daemon-less" are the same sentence in two
directions. The cost row is also the only published price of the hop, and it is paid per
turn, which is the axis tezgah's hooks do not spend from.
