## Detailed Report: Adaptive Influence Graphs for Failure Attribution in Multi-Agent Systems

This report provides a detailed analysis of the research paper "Adaptive Influence Graphs for Failure Attribution in Multi-Agent Systems" by Bakish et al. (2026). It covers the authors, the research context, objectives, methodology, findings, and the significance of the work.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development and deployment of multi-agent Large Language Model (LLM) systems are increasing, yet diagnosing failures in these complex systems remains challenging. When these systems fail, human engineers typically inspect lengthy, interleaved logs to pinpoint the initial error, a process that becomes costly as systems grow in complexity and operational duration. This challenge has led to a growing research focus on automated failure attribution, which involves identifying the agent and the specific step responsible for derailing an execution. The Who&When benchmark (Zhang et al., 2025b) formalizes this problem.

Prior work in multi-agent failure attribution has explored two main directions. One line of research focuses on enhancing the reasoning procedures applied to execution traces, often through multi-stage diagnosis pipelines (e.g., Zhang et al., 2025b; Zhu et al., 2025; West et al., 2025). These methods primarily manipulate how an LLM processes a largely fixed, flat trace interface, sometimes adding minor modifications like step indexing or context compression. A complementary approach involves restructuring the trace before attribution. This includes methods that convert log records into structured triples for spectrum analysis (Ge et al., 2025), derive data-dependency graphs (Ma et al., 2025), or encode steps into feature vectors for sequential models (Zhu et al., 2026). Some recent methods construct structured dependency representations and search them for failure sources, such as CHIEF (Wang et al., 2026b), which reconstructs flat logs into hierarchical causal graphs using predefined hierarchies and edge schemas.

The current work distinguishes itself by observing that human engineers rarely debug systems by reading raw logs sequentially. Instead, they rely on observability tools that organize execution traces around components, actions, and dependencies, facilitating targeted navigation. The authors hypothesize that LLMs could benefit from a similar paradigm. Unlike existing methods that either use fixed trace interfaces or restructure them with predefined policies, this research explores the idea that failure attribution is partly an "interface-design problem." It proposes that performance depends not only on the diagnosing model but also on how the trace is represented and adaptively traversed. This approach bridges the gap between structured trace representation and dynamic, agent-directed exploration, drawing inspiration from human debugging practices in complex software systems.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to advance automated failure attribution in multi-agent LLM systems, aiming to reduce the reliance on human engineers for debugging and enable more scalable system development and deployment. The specific problem addressed is the precise localization of the failure-responsible agent and the decisive error step within a failed execution trace, as defined by the Who&When benchmark.

The core motivation for this work stems from the observation that current automated attribution methods often treat execution traces as largely fixed, linear transcripts or apply predetermined restructuring rules. This contrasts with human debugging practices, where engineers leverage sophisticated observability tools that transform raw logs into navigable, structured representations (e.g., through spans, attributes, and dependency trees) to facilitate targeted investigation.

Based on this insight, the authors propose a central hypothesis: **failure attribution is partly an interface-design problem, depending not only on the diagnosing model but also on how the execution trace is represented and traversed.** To test this hypothesis, the research sets forth several key objectives:

1.  **Systematically Evaluate Trace Representations:** To quantify the impact of different levels of trace representation, the authors aim to evaluate a "representation ladder" ranging from raw logs to progressively richer structures: structured logs, Influence Graphs, and Adaptive Influence Graphs. This evaluation initially holds the "reader" model fixed (single-call reading) to isolate the benefits of representation alone.
2.  **Assess Agent-Directed Traversal:** A further objective is to evaluate the additional performance gains achieved when the LLM reader is empowered with agentic capabilities to dynamically traverse the structured trace representation, selectively inspecting underlying log details on demand, rather than processing the entire serialized representation in a single call.
3.  **Develop Adaptive Influence Graphs (AIGs):** The research aims to introduce AIGs, a novel two-stage agentic framework. This framework consists of an agentic builder that adaptively constructs a structured influence graph for each failed trace, and an independent agentic reader that navigates this graph to identify the critical error. A key aspect of the builder is its ability to jointly determine node boundaries and inheritance topology per trace, moving beyond fixed construction policies.
4.  **Establish Benchmark Performance:** A critical objective is to evaluate the performance of AIGs on the standard Who&When benchmark, comparing its exact agent and step accuracy against existing state-of-the-art methods to establish a new performance baseline.
5.  **Analyze Mechanisms of Improvement:** The study aims to analyze where AIGs yield improvements in attribution, specifically examining how the adaptive builder constructs richer graphs and how the agentic reader utilizes these graphs to focus its evidence gathering and verification process.

By addressing these objectives, the research seeks to empirically support its hypothesis and demonstrate that intelligent interface design for LLM agents can significantly enhance their diagnostic capabilities in complex multi-agent systems.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on Adaptive Influence Graphs (AIGs), a two-stage agentic framework for failure attribution. This framework comprises a "builder" stage that constructs a structured graph representation from a failed trace and a "reader" stage that navigates this graph to pinpoint the failure.

**4.1 Trace Representations**

To evaluate the impact of trace representation, the authors define a four-level "representation ladder," progressively adding structure:

1.  **Raw log:** The baseline, consisting of the verbatim sequence of steps `(a_t, s_t)` where `a_t` is the acting agent and `s_t` is the log content at step `t`.
2.  **Structured log:** An LLM-free, deterministic builder groups consecutive steps from the same agent into single nodes, forming a chain in execution order. Each node contains the verbatim steps of an agent's uninterrupted turn. This representation makes execution structure explicit without adding semantic abstraction.
3.  **Influence Graph (IG):** This representation enriches the structured log. An LLM, in a single pass, adds a four-field abstraction `⟨summary, input, output, authorship⟩` to each node. It also adds directed "inheritance edges" between nodes. An edge is drawn from an earlier node to a later one if the later node reused part of the earlier node's output in a way related to the failure, with annotations `inherited` (what was carried over) and `effect` (how it went wrong downstream).
4.  **Adaptive Influence Graph (AIG):** This is the most sophisticated representation, where an agentic builder adaptively constructs the graph.
    *   **Agentic Builder:** Operates from an empty graph. It uses two families of tools:
        *   `T_log` (Inspection tools): Allow the builder to query the raw trajectory at various granularities (e.g., `get_overview`, `read_step_range`, `search`).
        *   `T_build` (Construction tools): Enable creation, modification, and deletion of nodes and inheritance edges (e.g., `add_block`, `add_inheritance_note`, `modify_block`).
    *   **Node Types:** Nodes are grounded in one or more raw-log steps and assigned a type from a fixed vocabulary (task, orchestrator, agent, conclusion), each dictating specific fields (e.g., agent nodes include `input`, `output`, `authorship`). This allows for variable granularity in node definition (e.g., merging multiple raw steps).
    *   **Critic-Refiner Loop:** After the builder submits a graph, a critic audits it for validity. This includes programmatic checks for structural properties (e.g., connectivity, acyclicity, one root, one sink) and semantic checks for consistency with the raw log. A refiner applies minimal edits to resolve violations, with a maximum of three rounds (R=3).

**4.2 Reading Approaches**

Two distinct readers are evaluated to understand the impact of traversal strategy:

1.  **Single-call reading:** The entire serialized representation (raw log, structured log, IG, or AIG) is provided as input to a single LLM call, which then produces the failure attribution. This reader is used for ablating the representation ladder.
2.  **Agentic graph reading:** This reader accesses the finalized graph `G` and selectively inspects the underlying raw trajectory `τ` using `T_log` tools. It starts from a candidate node identified from the graph and iteratively works backward along incoming inheritance edges. The reader transfers "blame" to an upstream node only when the raw log confirms that the current node's output exhibits the downstream error described by the edge's `effect` annotation. This process continues until no further incoming inheritance edge can explain the observed failure. The reader then resolves the selected node to an exact agent and step using its `step_refs` and the raw log, submitting `(ˆa, ˆt)`.

**4.3 Implementation Details**

*   **Platform:** Agents for both stages were built using the Strands Agents SDK v1.37.
*   **LLM Backbones:** Opus-5, Sonnet-4, DeepSeek-V3.2 (DS-V3.2), and GPT-5.6-sol were used as backbone models, accessed via the Amazon Bedrock Converse API. No task-specific fine-tuning was applied.
*   **Evaluation Metrics:** The primary metrics were exact agent accuracy and exact step accuracy on both partitions of the Who&When benchmark (Algorithm-Generated and Hand-Crafted). Additionally, performance under relaxed tolerance windows (±n steps) was reported.

This modular approach allows for systematic isolation and evaluation of the contributions of both structured trace representation and agent-directed traversal to the efficacy of failure attribution.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several key findings regarding the effectiveness of Adaptive Influence Graphs (AIGs) and the importance of trace representation and traversal for failure attribution.

**5.1 Who&When Benchmark Performance**

*   **Algorithm-Generated Partition:** AIGs, when paired with Opus-5, achieved a step accuracy of 55.20% and an agent accuracy of 71.20%. This establishes a new state of the art on this partition, surpassing the previous best reported step accuracy of 51.60% by 3.6 percentage points. The system also localized 84.00% of failures within a ±3-step window.
*   **Consistency Across Models:** The performance gains were not limited to Opus-5. AIGs with GPT-5.6-sol (54.76% step accuracy), Sonnet-4 (53.97%), and DS-V3.2 (53.17%) also exceeded the prior state of the art. In matched-model comparisons, AIGs consistently outperformed existing methods like CHIEF (by 9.96 points with Opus-5) and RAFFLES (by 2.37 points with Sonnet-4), indicating robustness across different LLM backbones.
*   **Hand-Crafted Partition:** On this more challenging partition, AIGs with Opus-5 achieved 29.31% step accuracy, matching top reported results without ground-truth access. However, their agent accuracy (63.79%) was slightly lower than CHIEF's (72.41%) on this specific partition.

**5.2 The Interface Ladder Ablations**

A controlled ablation study on the Algorithm-Generated partition (using Opus-5) isolated the contributions of different trace representations and reading strategies:

*   **Explicit Structure Improves Attribution:** Moving from raw logs (46.40% step accuracy) to structured logs (48.80%) yielded a 2.4 percentage point increase. This demonstrates that simply grouping consecutive steps from the same agent, making execution structure explicit, contributes to improved accuracy. Similar, larger gains were observed with Sonnet-4 and DS-V3.2, suggesting that Opus-5 inherently recovers some of this structure from raw logs.
*   **Influence Structure Adds Further Gains:** Enriching structured logs into an Influence Graph (IG) further raised step accuracy to 52.00%. This gain, achieved with a single-call reader, indicates the value of abstracting node actions and adding explicit inheritance edges that trace the propagation of outputs.
*   **Agent-Directed Traversal Unlocks Adaptive Graph:** When the adaptively constructed AIG was flattened and processed by a single-call reader, step accuracy was 50.40%. However, when the agentic reader was given control to traverse the same AIG, accuracy increased by 4.8 points to 55.20%. This indicates that the AIG functions effectively as a "high-level map" that guides the reader's targeted inspection, rather than merely as a richer static prompt. Overall, adaptive construction paired with agent-directed traversal improved exact step accuracy by 8.8 points over raw-log reading.

**5.3 Analysis of Gains and System Behavior**

*   **Localization Deeper in the Trace:** AIGs primarily improve localization of failures occurring later in the execution trace, where symptoms can obscure origins. For failures at step 5 or later, Sonnet-4's exact accuracy rose from 10.8% with raw-log reading to 40.5% with AIGs. This suggests AIGs effectively help trace downstream consequences back to earlier sources.
*   **Robustness under Relaxed Tolerance:** AIGs consistently performed better than baselines across various relaxed localization windows (e.g., ±1, ±2, ±3 steps), indicating that even when not pinpointing the exact step, they often localize the failure nearby.
*   **Adaptive Graph Characteristics:** The agentic builder in AIGs produced richer, variable-granularity graphs compared to fixed IGs. AIGs had more nodes (9.5 vs. 7.5) and edges (13.5 vs. 9.0) on average, resulting in denser representations. They also exhibited higher maximum hub out-degrees (3.6 vs. 1.8), indicating identification of upstream actions influencing multiple downstream steps. The adaptive builder also merged longer sequences of same-agent steps into single nodes (2.8 vs. 1.6 steps) and exposed longer-range influence with greater mean cross-range distances (3.6 vs. 3.1 steps) for inheritance links.
*   **Reader Interaction Profile:** The agentic reader demonstrated focused evidence gathering. In 61.3% of executions, the first raw step inspected by the reader matched the final submitted step, and 48.4% of inspected steps were within one step of the prediction. However, the reader rarely accepted the graph's claims without verification, investigating multiple raw steps in 86.5% of executions and committing solely from the graph in only 1.6%. This indicates that the graph serves as a high-level map that guides a selective verification process against the original log.

In summary, the findings empirically support the hypothesis that both the representation of the trace and the method of its traversal significantly impact failure attribution. AIGs combine adaptive graph construction with agent-directed traversal to achieve state-of-the-art performance by providing a navigable interface that helps LLMs efficiently localize errors.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents Adaptive Influence Graphs (AIGs) as a framework that reframes multi-agent system failure attribution as an interface-design challenge, moving beyond solely improving reasoning over static or pre-structured logs. This perspective holds several significant implications and potential impacts:

**6.1 Paradigm Shift in LLM Debugging**
The core contribution is the systematic demonstration that the *interface* through which an LLM understands and explores an execution trace is critical to its diagnostic performance. By applying observability practices—transforming a raw trace into a structured, navigable object—to automated attribution, the work suggests a paradigm shift. Instead of treating LLMs as black boxes that consume text, this approach views them as agents that can interact with and explore structured data, similar to how human engineers use debugging tools.

**6.2 Enhanced Diagnostic Capabilities for Multi-Agent Systems**
AIGs establish a new state of the art on the Algorithm-Generated Who&When benchmark, with significant improvements in exact step accuracy. This practical advancement indicates that designing adaptive, navigable interfaces can substantially enhance LLMs' ability to localize critical errors. Such improved diagnostic capabilities are essential for the robust and scalable development and deployment of increasingly complex multi-agent LLM systems in real-world applications.

**6.3 Complementary Roles of Builder and Reader Agents**
The analysis highlights the complementary roles of the agentic builder and reader. The builder adaptively constructs a graph that explicitly exposes action-level granularity and long-range influence patterns. The reader then leverages this structure as a high-level map to focus its search and selectively verify claims against the underlying raw log. This division of labor allows each agent to specialize, leading to a more efficient and effective attribution process.

**6.4 Potential for Broader Application**
The principles of adaptive graph construction and agent-directed traversal could extend beyond failure attribution to other complex LLM system analysis tasks. Any scenario requiring an LLM to understand and reason over long, interleaved, or interdependent execution histories (e.g., performance optimization, security auditing, behavior analysis) might benefit from similar interface designs that facilitate targeted exploration and evidence gathering.

**6.5 Limitations and Ethical Considerations**
The authors acknowledge several limitations. The evaluation is primarily on the Who&When benchmark, raising questions about generalizability to other multi-agent frameworks or task distributions. The benchmark's partially observable setting (only agent outputs are visible) means the builder cannot ground nodes in inputs or tool logs, which could offer further improvements. The graph's internal structure (nodes, edges) is validated against downstream accuracy and a critic, rather than against gold annotations for graph topology. Finally, AIGs incur higher token costs due to the build stage and critic-refiner loop, suggesting their use is most justified in contexts where reliable diagnosis outweighs this overhead.

From an ethical standpoint, the work focuses on diagnostic utility for developers. The authors emphasize that outputs from any attribution method are imperfect and should not be treated as definitive assignments of blame, particularly in settings where incorrect attributions could have significant negative consequences. The research utilizes publicly available data and does not involve human subjects, personal data, or user-generated content beyond the benchmark datasets.
