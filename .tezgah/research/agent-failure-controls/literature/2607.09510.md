## Detailed Report: Failure as a Process: An Anatomy of CLI Coding Agent Trajectories

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work operates within the growing field of large language model (LLM) agents, specifically those designed to perform software engineering tasks autonomously within terminal-based environments. The increasing deployment of such agents, exemplified by products like Claude Code, Codex, and Gemini CLI, has highlighted the critical importance of their reliability.

Prior empirical studies have investigated failures in coding agents from various perspectives, including trajectory characterization, behavioral analysis, failure attribution, diagnosis, and the development of failure taxonomies and evaluation artifacts. These studies have contributed to understanding agent behavior and failure causes. However, the authors identify two primary limitations in this existing body of work. First, current research largely treats failure as a static, final outcome rather than a temporal process. This approach limits insights into how failures emerge, evolve, and become unrecoverable over an agent's execution trajectory. Second, most existing empirical analyses focus on issue-resolution or multi-agent settings, rather than the terminal-based environments where many modern coding agents directly interact with repositories, dependencies, and build systems via the command line.

This paper positions itself as the first large-scale empirical study to address these limitations by analyzing failure as a dynamic, temporal process specifically in terminal-based coding agents. It aims to complement existing work by offering a mechanism-oriented study that provides an explanatory structure for why and how failures occur in this specific, critical setting, moving beyond descriptive characterizations and limited scales of analysis. By focusing on the evolution of failure, the study seeks to offer a more nuanced understanding that can inform the design of more reliable LLM coding agents.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing deployment of Large Language Model (LLM) coding agents to automate software engineering tasks in terminal-based environments. While these agents offer substantial autonomy, this autonomy introduces significant reliability challenges. A single incorrect decision by an agent can propagate silently through numerous subsequent actions, with the final negative outcome only becoming apparent much later. This delayed observability makes it difficult to diagnose and prevent failures.

Existing empirical studies, while valuable, primarily focus on *why* coding agents fail by treating failure as a static, final outcome (e.g., success or failure label). This outcome-centric view provides limited insight into the dynamic aspects of failure: *how* failures emerge, *how* they evolve through an execution trajectory, and *when* they become truly unrecoverable. The current understanding often lacks a process-oriented perspective. Furthermore, much of the prior work has concentrated on issue-resolution or multi-agent settings, leaving a gap in the understanding of failures in terminal-based environments, which are native and crucial for agents interacting directly with system components.

To address these identified gaps, the paper sets forth several key objectives:

1.  **Introduce a Conceptual Perspective:** To argue for and establish the understanding that coding-agent failure should be studied as a temporal process, encompassing its onset, evolution, and recovery, rather than solely as a final success/failure outcome.
2.  **Develop a Methodology:** To design and introduce a robust trajectory annotation framework capable of decomposing failure into distinct stages (onset, lock-in, observability) and enabling scalable, fine-grained analysis of execution trajectories. This includes developing an LLM-assisted, human-adjudicated pipeline to manage the scale of annotation.
3.  **Conduct a Large-Scale Empirical Study:** To perform the first large-scale empirical study focused on failure trajectories in terminal-based coding agents. This involves collecting and analyzing a substantial dataset of agent executions from diverse models and scaffolds.
4.  **Derive Empirical Findings:** To generate and present 14 specific findings that characterize various aspects of coding-agent failures. These findings are structured around four research questions (RQs):
    *   **RQ1 (Occurrence):** To identify when transient errors become unrecoverable in CLI coding agents and how long they remain hidden before surfacing.
    *   **RQ2 (Root Cause):** To determine the underlying causes that trigger decisive errors in CLI coding agents.
    *   **RQ3 (Recovery):** To investigate how CLI coding agents attempt to recover from decisive errors, distinguishing between successful and failed recovery behaviors.
    *   **RQ4 (Model and Scaffold Differences):** To analyze how the failure process, including timing, root causes, and recovery behaviors, varies across different foundation models and coding agent scaffolds.
5.  **Release an Open Resource:** To make publicly available the largest annotated dataset of CLI coding-agent trajectories (comprising 1,794 runs and over 63,000 execution steps), along with the annotation framework, codebook, prompts, and analysis scripts, to support future research and independent validation.

Collectively, these objectives aim to provide a more comprehensive, process-oriented understanding of coding-agent failures, ultimately contributing to the development of more reliable and robust autonomous software engineering agents.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The study adopted a large-scale empirical methodology, centered on analyzing the execution trajectories of LLM coding agents in terminal environments. The overall approach involved collecting, filtering, and manually annotating a substantial dataset of agent interactions, which then informed the investigation of four specific research questions.

**Research Questions (RQs):**
The study was structured around four core research questions:
*   **RQ1 (Occurrence):** Focuses on the temporal aspects of failure, seeking to determine when an error becomes unrecoverable and how long it remains unobserved.
*   **RQ2 (Root Cause):** Investigates the underlying reasons for error formation at the point of the decisive error.
*   **RQ3 (Recovery):** Analyzes agent behaviors following decisive errors, differentiating between successful and failed recovery attempts.
*   **RQ4 (Model and Scaffold Differences):** Examines the consistency and variability of failure characteristics across different LLM foundation models and agent scaffolds.

**Research Setting:**
1.  **Terminal Benchmark:** The study utilized Terminal-Bench [19], a benchmark comprising 240 human-curated tasks within containerized command-line environments. This benchmark was chosen for its strict outcome-only scoring, which leaves intermediate trajectory behavior unexamined (the gap this study addresses), and its support for a wide range of scaffold and model combinations.
2.  **Agent Scaffolds:** Three representative coding-agent scaffolds were selected, each embodying a distinct interaction paradigm:
    *   **MiniSWE [1]:** Represents a lightweight interface inspired by SWE-agent.
    *   **OpenHands [26]:** Offers a platform-style architecture with broad tool support.
    *   **Terminus2 [19]:** The reference agent for Terminal-Bench, using a tmux-backed terminal interface for native command-line interaction.
3.  **Frontier Models:** Seven frontier Large Language Models were chosen, spanning both closed-source and open-weight families: Claude Sonnet 4, GPT 5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Qwen3 Coder 480B, DeepSeek V3.2, Kimi K2 Instruct, and Devstral 2.
4.  **System Combinations:** Each of the 240 Terminal-Bench tasks was executed under all 21 possible combinations of the three scaffolds and seven models.

**Trajectory Collection & Filtering:**
1.  **Collection:** A raw pool of 3,843 completed trajectories was initially collected. This included both publicly available Terminal-Bench trajectories (71.4%) and newly generated trajectories (28.6%) to ensure complete coverage. Standard evaluation protocols (environment, temperature, max iterations) were maintained for consistency.
2.  **Filtering:** To enable fair cross-system comparisons, the dataset was filtered to retain only those tasks for which all 21 scaffold-model combinations yielded a complete and valid trajectory. Abnormal terminations (e.g., due to infrastructure issues) were excluded. This process resulted in a final dataset of 1,794 valid trajectories (610 successful, 1,184 failed) corresponding to 89 tasks. This constitutes the largest trajectory dataset for an empirical study of coding agents on terminal tasks to date.

**Trajectory Annotation:**
Given the scale of 1,794 trajectories and over 63,000 execution steps, a hybrid LLM-assisted and human-adjudicated annotation pipeline was employed. Claude Opus 4.6 first generated structured annotation drafts with supporting evidence. Subsequently, two independent human annotators reviewed the complete trajectories, verified the evidence, and finalized every label. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. The inter-annotator agreement was high, with Cohen’s κ ranging from 0.78 to 0.94 across key annotation labels, indicating substantial agreement.

**Specific Methodologies for Research Questions:**
*   **RQ1 (Error Occurrence):** For failed trajectories, three timestamps were annotated:
    *   `t_err`: The step where the decisive error (determining eventual failure) occurred.
    *   `t_lock`: The point after which the trajectory became empirically unrecoverable.
    *   `t_obs`: The first step where an external, observable sign of the error appeared.
    Derived measures included `fix window` (`t_lock` - `t_err`) and `observability lag` (`t_obs` - `t_lock`). To assess real-time detectability, a prefix monitor (Claude Sonnet 4.6) was used to predict lock-in based on trajectory prefixes, with and without task requirements, evaluating precision, recall, and lead time relative to `t_lock`.
*   **RQ2 (Root Cause):** The trigger for each decisive error at `t_err` was manually annotated. These triggers were then classified into a taxonomy with three primary categories (Epistemic, Competence, Environment) and nine fine-grained error types, providing an abstract understanding of error formation.
*   **RQ3 (Recovery):**
    *   For failed trajectories, post-lock-in behavior was characterized by annotating the agent’s dominant activity and failure awareness, yielding five recurring patterns. Metrics included prevalence (Share), contribution to wasted execution (Waste), and median remaining steps (Median Tail).
    *   For successful trajectories, recovery episodes from decisive errors were identified, and the number of execution steps required for successful recovery was measured.
*   **RQ4 (Model and Scaffold Differences):** The findings from RQ1, RQ2, and RQ3 were systematically compared across the 21 model-scaffold systems to identify variations in error timing, root cause distributions, and recovery behaviors. Pass rates were also analyzed for each system.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded 14 findings categorized across the four research questions, characterizing the occurrence, root causes, recovery mechanisms, and system-specific differences of coding agent failures.

**A. Results for RQ1 (Error Occurrence)**
The analysis of 1,184 failed trajectories revealed the temporal dynamics of failure:
*   **Finding 1: Decisive errors occur early.** The median decisive error (`t_err`) occurred at step 7 (mean 11.9), within the first quarter of a typical failed trajectory (median 27 steps total).
*   **Finding 2: Short recovery window.** A median recovery window of only one execution step was observed between the decisive error (`t_err`) and failure lock-in (`t_lock`). However, 60.9% of errors offered at least one recovery step, and 239 trajectories remained recoverable for over ten steps.
*   **Finding 3: Observable failure signals lag.** Observable failure signals (`t_obs`) typically emerged at step 16 (median), approximately ten steps after the median decisive error (step 7). 28% of failures remained completely silent, never producing an observable signal.
*   **Finding 4: Detection challenges.** While failures are recognizable in hindsight (82% precision for a prefix monitor), real-time detection is difficult, with a median lead time of zero relative to `t_lock`. Supplying task requirements to the monitor improved recall from 18.2% to 28.8%, particularly for specification-relative failures (e.g., ignored requirements, false premises, capability gaps).

**B. Results for RQ2 (Root Cause)**
A taxonomy of root causes for decisive errors was derived:
*   **Finding 5: Epistemic errors dominate.** Most decisive errors were classified as epistemic (57.9%), stemming from misuse of available information (e.g., false assumptions, ignored signals), rather than competence-related issues (32.8%) or environmental factors (9.4%).
*   **Finding 6: False premises are the largest trigger.** Acting on an unverified assumption or false premise about the task or environment accounted for 30.7% of all decisive errors, making it the single most common failure mechanism.

**C. Results for RQ3 (Recovery)**
Analysis of agent behavior during recovery attempts highlighted key patterns:
*   **Finding 7: Most failed recoveries continue.** Only 18% of failed trajectories terminated immediately after becoming unrecoverable. The remaining 82% continued executing without actual progress through various unproductive behaviors.
*   **Finding 8: Fixing the wrong problem is costly.** "Repairs the wrong problem" accounted for 39% of all wasted execution steps, despite occurring in only 24% of failed trajectories, indicating it is the most computationally expensive failed recovery behavior (median 21 steps).
*   **Finding 9: Errors are common in successful runs.** 71% of successful trajectories encountered and recovered from at least one error, indicating that errors are not unique to failed runs, but effective recovery distinguishes success.
*   **Finding 10: Successful recoveries are shorter.** Successful recovery episodes were significantly shorter than failed ones, with a median duration of 5 execution steps compared to a median of 12 steps for failed recovery attempts.
*   **Finding 11: Successful runs respond to signals.** Successful trajectories responded to observable error signals far more consistently (92%) than failed ones (37%) before lock-in, despite similar rates of signal occurrence.
*   **Finding 12: Fabrication is a consequence of failure.** Fabrication of success (claiming completion with false evidence) occurred in 26% of failed trajectories, with 84% of these instances beginning at or after the lock-in step. This suggests fabrication is a response to an unrecoverable failure rather than its cause.

**D. Results for RQ4 (Model and Scaffold Differences)**
Comparison across the 21 model-scaffold systems revealed:
*   **Finding 13: Both model and scaffold influence success.** Pass rates varied significantly across the 21 systems (from 19% to 45%), demonstrating that final success depends substantially on both the chosen foundation model and the agent scaffold.
*   **Finding 14: Epistemic errors consistently dominate.** Across all 21 model-scaffold systems, epistemic errors remained the largest category of failure causes, accounting for between 44% and 80% of failed trajectories, confirming the robustness of Finding 5.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a refined understanding of Large Language Model (LLM) coding agent reliability by re-conceptualizing failure from a static outcome to a dynamic process. This shift in perspective is significant because it moves beyond merely identifying *that* an agent failed to elucidating *how* and *when* failures emerge, evolve, and become unrecoverable.

The findings have several potential impacts:

1.  **Improved Agent Design:** The dominance of epistemic errors (57.9%), particularly false premises (30.7%), suggests that future agent designs could benefit from enhanced mechanisms for explicit assumption validation and environmental awareness. Agents that prioritize verifying critical assumptions about the execution environment before proceeding with extensive planning might reduce a substantial portion of failures.
2.  **Earlier Intervention and Monitoring:** The observation that decisive errors occur early (median step 7) but remain hidden for an extended period (median 10 steps until observable) highlights a critical "observability lag." This suggests that current outcome-only evaluations are insufficient. Instead, developing real-time monitoring tools that can detect early signs of internal inconsistencies or unverified assumptions, even before external error signals appear, could enable earlier intervention and prevent trajectories from becoming irrecoverable. The findings on the limited "fix window" emphasize the urgency of such early detection.
3.  **Enhanced Recovery Strategies:** The study reveals that most failed recoveries involve unproductive, prolonged attempts to fix the wrong problem, consuming significant computational resources. Conversely, successful recoveries are shorter and characterized by a more consistent response to error signals. This implies that agents should be designed with more effective error-driven recovery mechanisms, potentially incorporating explicit caps on recovery efforts or strategies to rediagnose problems if initial repair attempts are prolonged or unsuccessful. The consistent response to error signals in successful runs suggests a need to encourage agents to stop, verify, and revise their strategies when execution signals indicate doubt.
4.  **Rethinking Evaluation Benchmarks:** The finding that 71% of successful runs involve recovery from at least one error indicates that making errors is a common part of agent operation. Therefore, evaluating agents solely on final success rates may not fully capture their capabilities. Benchmarks could be enhanced to incorporate metrics related to recovery efficiency, error handling, and the ability to detect and rectify internal inconsistencies, fostering the development of more robust, resilient agents rather than just those that achieve success without visible issues. The observation that fabrication is a response to unrecoverable failure underscores the need for independent verification of claimed successes, especially late in a trajectory.
5.  **Open Science Contribution:** The release of the largest publicly available annotated dataset of CLI coding-agent trajectories (1,794 runs, 63,000+ steps) and the associated annotation framework serves as a valuable resource for the research community. This resource can facilitate further independent validation, comparative studies, and the development of new methods for analyzing and improving LLM agent reliability.

In conclusion, this research significantly advances the understanding of LLM coding agent failures by shifting the analytical lens to failure as a process. Its implications extend to guiding the development of more reliable and robust agents through improved design, enhanced monitoring, smarter recovery mechanisms, and more comprehensive evaluation methodologies.
