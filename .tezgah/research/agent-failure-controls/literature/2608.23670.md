## Detailed Report: Automata from Agent Traces: Failure and Next-Step Prediction

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The research was conducted by Seonglae Cho, Franklin Cardenoso Fernandez, Umar Mohammed, Ilham Wicaksono, who are affiliated with Holistic AI, and Seonglae Cho (also corresponding author), Zekun Wu, Kleyton Da Costa, and Adriano Koshiyama, who are affiliated with University College London. Franklin Cardenoso Fernandez also holds an affiliation with PUC-Rio.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The deployment of Large Language Model (LLM)-based agents in increasingly complex, multi-step tasks has introduced challenges related to their operational opacity. As these agents resolve issues, navigate digital environments, and orchestrate multi-agent pipelines, their underlying behavioral structure often remains implicit within long, unstructured execution traces. This opacity impedes essential practices such as safety auditing, debugging of bottleneck states, and real-time monitoring for behavioral drift in production environments.

Existing approaches to understanding agent behavior frequently operate on a per-trace basis, or exclusively analyze successful traces, thus failing to capture a holistic, cross-run behavioral topology. Methods like AgentSpec and ShieldAgent enforce safety policies, while AgentMonitor uses step-level features to predict performance. ProbGuard learns Discrete-Time Markov Chains (DTMCs) for runtime safety filtering, but its effectiveness can be limited without hand-crafted unsafe predicates. Concurrent trajectory anomaly detectors provide anomaly scores, but typically do not offer a compact structural representation that can unify multiple prediction goals. Previous work on behavioral abstractions, such as Agent Workflow Memory (AWM), Reflexion, ETO, and Reasoning-Bank, focuses on extracting linear patterns or learning from failures without yielding comprehensive structural models with state abstraction. Approaches in FSM extraction like AFlow, MetaAgent, and StateFlow often rely on task descriptions or manual specification.

This work addresses these limitations by framing behavioral recovery as an inverse problem: given a corpus of execution traces, reconstruct a Finite State Machine (FSM) that explains the observed behavior. It distinguishes itself by providing a learned, compact, and interpretable structural model from positive-only examples (agent traces), which unifies tasks such as workflow memory, next-step prediction, failure prediction, and runtime monitoring. The authors emphasize that the bounded nature of LLM agent activity alphabets (6-42 symbols) makes this problem tractable, enabling the creation of small, statistically informative FSMs without requiring learning hyperparameters or negative examples, a known challenge in grammatical inference. This differentiates it from grammatical inference algorithms like RPNI, EDSM, and Alergia, which may overfit, produce non-interpretable states, or require negative examples, and from process mining techniques that often yield over-general "flower models."

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The proliferation of LLM-based agents into domains requiring longer reasoning chains and broader action spaces has led to an escalating risk of undetected failures. These agents generate complex execution traces where the behavioral structure, arising from the interaction between system prompts, available tools, and task distributions, is not explicitly defined. This implicit structure makes it challenging to ensure agent safety, diagnose operational issues, and monitor performance in deployment.

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized need for explicit and interpretable models of agent behavior. Without such models, safety auditing remains rudimentary, debugging bottleneck states becomes difficult, and monitoring for behavioral drift in production is largely reactive. Current methodologies are often insufficient as they either:
*   Operate at the individual trace level, missing overarching patterns.
*   Require manual specification or domain-specific knowledge to define expected workflows.
*   Are limited to analyzing only successful execution paths, thereby overlooking critical information from failures.

To address this, the key objectives of this work are:

1.  **Recover Shared Behavioral Structure:** To develop a method capable of collapsing an entire corpus of agent execution traces into a single, compact, and interpretable Finite State Machine (FSM). This FSM is intended to serve as a foundational structural substrate for otherwise complex and potentially unpredictable LLM agent behaviors.
2.  **Enable Next-Step Prediction:** To evaluate the effectiveness of the FSM's state context in predicting the agent's subsequent action, aiming to improve upon existing methods for agent workflow memory and next-step forecasting.
3.  **Facilitate Failure Prediction:** To demonstrate that features derived from the FSM (e.g., per-state behavioral metrics, cross-entropy anomaly scores) can effectively predict task success or failure from agent traces, including early detection capabilities from partial traces.
4.  **Support Runtime Monitoring:** To utilize the constructed FSM for creating an online monitoring system capable of detecting anomalous behavior and triggering early stopping of failing agent runs, thereby enhancing operational safety and efficiency.
5.  **Achieve Compactness and Interpretability:** To construct FSMs that are small in state count, statistically robust, and directly interpretable, making them practical for real-world auditing and monitoring without the need for complex, opaque models.
6.  **Ensure Robustness and Generalizability:** To verify the method's robustness to varying activity extraction granularities and to demonstrate the transferability of the FSMs and their derived features across different LLM models and agent domains.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to provide a model-agnostic structural primitive that enhances the safety, auditability, and operational intelligence of LLM-based agent deployments.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The methodology focuses on reconstructing a compact Finite State Machine (FSM) from a corpus of LLM agent execution traces, treating this as an inverse problem. The approach is designed to be deterministic, hyperparameter-free, and leverage the bounded nature of agent activity alphabets.

**Problem Formulation:**
An agent execution trace `τ` is defined as a sequence of messages `(m1, ..., mT)`. Each message `mt` is mapped to an activity symbol `at` from a finite alphabet `A` using an activity extraction function `ϕ`. The objective is to construct a deterministic FSM `M = (Q, A, δ, q0, Q)` from a corpus `D = {τ1, ..., τN}` of such activity sequences `σ(τ)`. All states in the constructed FSM are considered accepting. The quality of the FSM is quantified by "replay fitness," defined as `fit(σ, M) = k/T`, where `k` is the number of symbols consumed by `M` when replaying `σ`, and `T` is the total length of `σ`. Corpus fitness `Fit(D, M)` is the average `fit(σ, M)` across all traces in `D`.

**Activity Extraction (Section 3.2):**
To handle heterogeneous trace formats, a priority-based extraction mechanism is employed:
1.  **Tool Calls:** If a message contains a tool call field, the activity is the function name.
2.  **Action Tags:** If the content includes `[ACTION] description`, the activity is the action label.
3.  **Command Extraction:** For agents using code blocks, the first command token is extracted and mapped to a semantic category.
If none of these rules match, the activity defaults to a `role:content type` (e.g., `assistant:text`). The extraction process is deterministic and tailored to specific dataset formats. Robustness to different extraction granularities was verified, showing stability in replay fitness and failure prediction AUROC.

**FSM Construction (Section 3.3, Algorithm A.1):**
The construction proceeds in three steps:
1.  **Prefix Tree:** All activity sequences from the corpus are inserted into a trie. Each unique prefix in the trie represents a distinct state. This initial step results in perfect training fitness but a potentially large number of states.
2.  **Merge by Last Activity:** Trie states are merged based on a "last-activity right congruence." Specifically, two trie states `q` and `q'` are merged if `κ(q) = κ(q')`, where `κ(q)` is the activity on the edge leading into `q` (the root `qε` maps to `init`). This merging process aggregates traversal counts, forming a directly-follows automaton where cycles emerge from recurrent activities. This step yields `|A| + 1` states, one for each activity plus the initial state.
3.  **Rare-Transition Filtering:** Any merged transition observed exactly once in the corpus is removed, unless it is the *only* continuation from its source state. This step aims to prune infrequent digressions and is the only part of the construction that may reduce replay fitness.

**Construction and Convergence Guarantees (Section 3.4):**
*   **Fitness Preservation (Theorem 2):** The last-activity merge preserves training fitness; any trace accepted by the prefix tree is accepted by the merged FSM of Step 2, as merging only adds out-edges. Fitness loss is solely attributed to Step 3 filtering.
*   **Determinism and Compactness (Theorem 3):** The merge operation ensures the FSM is deterministic and results in `|Q| = |A| + 1` states. The construction is a deterministic function of the corpus, ensuring unique output for fixed data. It recovers the directly-follows automaton of observed traces.
*   **Complexity (Remark 4):** The total runtime for construction is linear in the corpus size, `O(N * T * |A|)`. Empirically, it completes in milliseconds.
*   **Convergence Guarantee (Proposition 5):** Under an i.i.d. trace assumption, the extracted FSM is guaranteed to converge to the population directly-follows automaton with high probability after a sufficient number of traces, bounded by `N ≥ (1/p_min) * ln(r/δ_f)`.

**Prediction via FSM State Conditioning (Section 3.5):**
For next-step prediction, the FSM provides state context `qt` to estimate `P(at | qt)` using Laplace-smoothed transition counts from training data. Higher-order context can be incorporated using prediction by partial matching (PPM). Predictive quality is evaluated using cross-entropy (CE).
*   **Consistency and Concentration (Proposition 6):** The compact state space of the FSM aggregates observations per state, enabling reliable probability estimation with a concentration bound for `P(·|q)`.
*   **Surprise as Log-Likelihood Ratio (Corollary 7):** The difference in trace cross-entropy between success and failure models provides an optimal statistic for distinguishing outcomes.
*   **Online Monitoring Regret (Proposition 8):** The compact `|Q|` facilitates a sub-linear regret bound for an online log-likelihood-ratio monitor, supporting efficient early stopping.

**Evaluation Metrics (Section 3.6):**
Beyond replay fitness and cross-entropy, evaluation includes:
*   **Precision:** The FSM's ability to reject invalid (random or permuted) traces.
*   **Compression:** The ratio of states in baseline automata to the FSM.
*   **Stability:** Variance in FSM structure across random train/test splits.

**Experimental Setup (Section 4.1):**
Experiments were conducted on twelve public datasets covering eight agent domains, with alphabet sizes ranging from 6 to 42 symbols. An 80/20 train/test split was used. Nine datasets had outcome labels for failure prediction. Baselines included prominent algorithms from automata learning (RPNI, EDSM, Alergia, k-Tails), Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), process mining (Alpha, Inductive, Heuristic Miners), and workflow extraction (Agent Workflow Memory - AWM). All baselines received only positive examples.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the construction and utility of FSMs derived from LLM agent traces:

**FSM Construction and Properties:**
*   **Compactness and High Fitness:** The constructed FSMs were compact, ranging from 7 to 43 states, while maintaining high replay fitness of ≥0.997 on held-out data across all twelve datasets. This represented a 15–3,036× compression ratio compared to RPNI, a common grammatical inference algorithm, which often produced orders of magnitude more states (e.g., 59,510 states for SWE-agent) or timed out. Alergia, another positive-only method, matched fitness but with 1.0–6.0× more states than the proposed FSM.
*   **Rapid Convergence:** FSM fitness rapidly converged, reaching ≥0.99 with only 5–15% of the training data. The state space stabilized later as rare activity patterns were observed.
*   **Structural Stability:** The FSM construction was deterministic and hyperparameter-free, ensuring a unique FSM for a given corpus. State counts remained stable across random train/test splits, indicating robustness, unlike RPNI whose state counts varied significantly.
*   **High Precision:** The FSM demonstrated high precision, rejecting 100% of random traces and ≥99.9% of permuted traces. It also rejected 77–100% of traces with plausible single-symbol mutations (substitution, insertion, adjacent swap), indicating that it captures meaningful sequential constraints such as turn-taking and tool-invocation rules. Process mining baselines, in contrast, often exhibited lower precision (0.00–0.80).
*   **Runtime Efficiency:** FSMs were constructed rapidly, typically within 1–110 ms, and per-trace replay took 0.003–0.015 ms, enabling real-time monitoring applications.

**Next-Step Prediction:**
*   **Improved Cross-Entropy:** Using FSM-state context for next-step prediction resulted in a 62% reduction in average cross-entropy (0.93 bits) compared to a Unigram baseline (2.44 bits). This FSM conditioning alone accounted for 83–99% of the total cross-entropy improvement from a uniform baseline to the best prediction method.
*   **Superior to Agent Workflow Memory (AWM):** When provided as context to an LLM next-action predictor (gpt-4.1-mini), the FSM's minimal context format outperformed AWM on all eight datasets, with gains ranging from +0.8 to +25.3 percentage points in top-1 accuracy. This advantage was attributed to the FSM's explicit representation of multi-step continuations and probabilities, as well as its ability to learn from all traces (not just successful ones, unlike AWM).
*   **Augmentation for Learned Models:** FSM state conditioning consistently improved next-step prediction across various learned models (e.g., logistic regression, ESN, MLP, GRU, Transformer), demonstrating that the FSM serves as a beneficial structural primitive rather than a competing approach.

**Failure Prediction:**
*   **High AUROC Scores:** FSM-derived features, including per-state visit frequencies, message lengths, error rates, and cross-entropy anomaly scores, achieved held-out AUROC scores up to 0.941. These features consistently outperformed raw trace statistics and lifted MLP/GRU/Transformer baselines on 20 of 21 dataset-architecture pairs.
*   **Early Detection:** The predictive signal for failure emerged early in traces; FSM features at 50% completion reached 92% of the full-trace AUROC, enabling early intervention.
*   **Structural Divergence:** Analysis revealed that successful and failed traces exhibited distinct structural patterns. For example, on SWE-agent, successful traces followed a focused path through 9 of 25 states, while failures spanned all 25 states with more chaotic exploration.
*   **Discriminative Quotient:** A label-aware FSM variant (FSM-D), using per-state KL divergence features, showed AUROC lifts up to +0.152, suggesting that divergences in outgoing transition distributions are discriminative of success/failure.

**Runtime Monitoring:**
*   **Effective Early Stopping:** An FSM-based online monitor, leveraging simple rules such as cycle-rate and minimum unique-state counts, achieved a rank-AUROC of 0.66 at the 25% trace checkpoint on four datasets. On SWE-agent, it triggered early stopping at 32% mean trace completion with 85.9% precision and 95.5% recall, saving 68% of remaining computation. At a high-precision operating point, it achieved 100% precision with zero false alarms.

**Cross-Model Transfer and Robustness:**
*   **Model-Invariant Topology:** A single FSM trained on combined traces from four different LLMs achieved perfect replay fitness on each model individually, suggesting that the behavioral topology is largely dictated by the deployment harness (tools, system prompt) rather than the specific LLM.
*   **Feature Transferability:** Failure prediction features derived from FSMs showed moderate cross-model transferability (mean cross-AUROC 0.786 vs. 0.877 self-AUROC), demonstrating a degree of model-agnosticism.
*   **Robust to Granularity:** FSM structure and predictive performance were robust across different activity extraction granularities, with minor variations in AUROC (less than 0.03) between coarse (role-only) and fine (tool-only) levels.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents a significant contribution to the understanding, auditing, and real-time management of LLM-based agent systems by providing a novel and effective method for extracting their behavioral structure. The core significance lies in demonstrating that despite the apparent complexity of LLM agent behavior, a compact and interpretable structural abstraction in the form of a Finite State Machine can be automatically derived from positive-only execution traces.

The potential impact of this work is multi-faceted:

*   **Enhanced Safety and Auditing:** The extracted FSMs offer an explicit, interpretable model of agent behavior, making the underlying logic of multi-step tasks transparent. This transparency is crucial for safety auditing, allowing developers and auditors to visualize expected workflows, identify unintended paths, and formally verify adherence to safety specifications. The FSM's ability to reject invalid traces provides a mechanism for enforcing safety constraints at runtime.
*   **Improved Runtime Monitoring and Early Intervention:** The capability for real-time, low-latency FSM replay (0.003–0.015 ms per trace step) enables the deployment of online monitors. These monitors can detect behavioral anomalies, such as agents getting stuck in loops or deviating from expected sequences, and trigger early stopping of failing runs. This saves computational resources, prevents potential negative consequences of agent failures, and improves the overall robustness of LLM agent deployments. The demonstrated sub-linear regret bound for the online monitor suggests practical efficiency gains over reactive monitoring.
*   **Streamlined Debugging and Development:** By providing a clear map of agent behavior, the FSM can expedite debugging efforts. Developers can easily identify bottleneck states, pinpoint where an agent deviates from a successful path, and characterize specific failure modes (e.g., distinguishing an agent "stuck in an edit loop" from one that "gave up early"). This understanding can inform agent design and prompt engineering, leading to more reliable and efficient agents.
*   **Unified Behavioral Intelligence:** The FSM serves as a single, foundational primitive that underpins multiple critical functions: workflow memory, next-step prediction, failure prediction, and runtime monitoring. This unification streamlines the development of agent intelligence systems, replacing potentially disparate and bespoke pipelines with a consistent structural model.
*   **Model-Agnostic Abstraction:** The finding that FSM topology is largely invariant to the specific LLM model used, and robust to extraction granularity, suggests that the behavioral structure is shaped more by the deployment environment (e.g., available tools, system prompts) than by the LLM itself. This provides a valuable model-agnostic abstraction that can generalize across different LLM architectures and versions.
*   **Advancement in Grammatical Inference:** The method contributes to grammatical inference by demonstrating a practical, deterministic, and hyperparameter-free approach to learning compact automata from positive-only examples, particularly in domains with bounded alphabets, overcoming some of the limitations of classical algorithms that struggle with sparsity or require negative examples.

While the current work acknowledges limitations such as the FSM capturing the directly-follows closure rather than the full generating language, and the need for some domain knowledge in activity extraction, its strong empirical results and theoretical guarantees establish the FSM as a deployable substrate for managing and understanding the increasingly complex world of LLM agents. Future work on fully automatic activity discovery and broader cross-domain transfer could further amplify this impact.
