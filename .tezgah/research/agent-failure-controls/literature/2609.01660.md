### Research Paper Report: How Fast Do Agents Rot? An Empirical Study of Long-Horizon Degradation in LLM Agents for Production Decision-Making

### 1. Authors and Institution(s)

The author of this paper is Shubhra Mittal, affiliated as an Independent Researcher and Principal Software Engineer at Microsoft AI.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a discrepancy between the reported success of large language model (LLM) agents on rapidly expanding benchmarks and their perceived unreliability in production deployments, particularly for long, multi-step workflows. Existing agent benchmarks, such as SWE-bench, WebArena, GAIA, and Tau-Bench, often report aggregate single-attempt success across a fixed task set. While some work, like Kwa et al. [9], tracks the time horizon of tasks AI systems can complete across successive model generations, this study distinguishes itself by isolating and characterizing the decay curve of success against horizon for specific models within a controlled environment, rather than observing trends over calendar time.

A parallel line of inquiry in the evaluation-rigor literature, including work by Kapoor et al. [10], highlights shortcomings in current agent benchmarking practices, such as cost-blindness and a lack of reporting on variance or compute budgets. This body of work motivates a more rigorous, reliability-oriented measurement approach, yet, to the author's knowledge, none of it resolves success against horizon as its primary analytical axis. This paper aims to close that specific gap by providing a mechanism-level measurement.

Furthermore, a significant body of literature explores performance degradation in LLMs related to context length and compositional complexity. Liu et al. [3] demonstrated performance degradation when relevant information is located in the middle of a long input ("Lost in the Middle"), while Levy et al. [16] investigated degradation with increasing input length. Dziri et al. [4] observed a sharp decay in accuracy with compositional complexity, and Laban et al. [11] reported significant performance drops in multi-turn conversational settings compared to single-turn interactions. This existing literature might predict context length as a primary driver of agent degradation. This study directly tests this prediction through its disentangling design and reports contradictory findings, implicating step count over context length. The paper also differentiates its "compressed regime" from synthetic long-context probes like RULER [14] and NoLiMa [18], which test retrieval at extreme lengths, by focusing on the loss of the agent's *own* prior reasoning scaffold when pruned.

By focusing on a directly measured, mechanism-level understanding of agent degradation across varying task horizons and disentangling the effects of step count and context length, this research provides a quantitative framework that complements and extends prior qualitative observations on error compounding in multi-agent systems, such as those taxonomized by Cemri et al. [12].

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this study stems from an observed "stubborn gap" between the high benchmark success rates of large language model (LLM) agents and their unreliability in long, multi-step workflows encountered in production deployments. While benchmarks often show steady improvements in agent capabilities, practitioners deploying these systems report unpredictable degradation when agents are tasked with sustaining many dependent steps in live environments.

The paper posits a specific hypothesis to explain this discrepancy: task horizon is a critical, overlooked factor. It proposes that if each step in a multi-step task succeeds independently with a probability *r* (where *r* is less than 1), then the probability of successfully completing a task of horizon *H* (i.e., *H* dependent steps) approximates *r^H*. This geometric decay implies that success remains high for short tasks but collapses rapidly as *H* increases. The motivation is that benchmarks predominantly sample short-to-medium horizons, which correspond to the flatter, higher-success portion of this decay curve, thereby obscuring the rapid degradation that occurs at the longer horizons typical of production workloads. The "production reliability gap" is thus hypothesized to be an expected mathematical consequence of task horizon rather than an unexplained anomaly.

The key objectives of this empirical study are:
1.  **Characterize the functional form of success against horizon:** To determine if task success indeed follows a geometric law or another pattern as the number of dependent steps increases.
2.  **Disentangle the driving cause of degradation:** To differentiate whether the primary cause of degradation is the absolute number of steps an agent must execute correctly or the increasing length of context it must process during these steps.
3.  **Provide mechanism-level measurements:** To move beyond aggregate leaderboard entries and offer resolved, mechanism-level insights into agent failure modes, responding to calls for more rigorous agent evaluation.
4.  **Quantify the benchmark-production gap:** To project measured reliability onto representative benchmark and production horizons, thereby providing a quantitative estimate of the difference in expected performance.
5.  **Inform practical guidelines:** To offer recommendations for practitioners responsible for agent orchestration and reliability at scale, as well as for designers of future agent benchmarks.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The study employs a controlled experimental design across multiple models, task families, and context regimes to empirically investigate agent degradation.

**4.1 Task Families**
Four synthetic task families were designed, each paired with a ground-truth simulator for exact, oracle-verifiable success, eliminating LLM-judge noise:
*   **Ledger:** Tests arithmetic working memory by requiring agents to maintain account balances through sequences of operations and report queried balances.
*   **Refchain:** Evaluates long-range retrieval by tasking agents to track a specific variable through a sequence of assignments amidst distractors and report its final value.
*   **Cipher:** Assesses procedural execution by requiring agents to apply a sequence of ordered string edits to a short string and report the result.
*   **ToolQA:** This is a genuinely agentic task where the agent must reach a target node a fixed number of hops along a hidden chain. It necessitates issuing its own inspection tool calls, as the full path cannot be pre-planned because each hop's target is revealed only by inspecting the previous node. This task uses an unmodified ReAct-style loop to reflect common deployment mechanisms.

**4.2 Context Regimes**
To disentangle the effects of step count from context length, three distinct context regimes were implemented for the streaming task families (Ledger, Refchain, Cipher) at a fixed horizon:
*   **Natural Regime:** The agent receives one instruction per turn, maintaining the full multi-turn history in its context.
*   **Compressed Regime:** The same number of operations is presented over the same number of turns, but the context is intentionally shortened via carried state and windowed history. This regime is designed to isolate the effect of context length on its own.
*   **Padded Regime:** All operations for a given horizon are delivered in a single turn. This regime aims to isolate the effect of the multi-step process itself, removing the multi-turn conversational structure.
The same underlying task instance was reused across all three regimes to enable paired comparisons and enhance statistical power.

**4.3 Models, Design, and Metrics**
The evaluation included nine instruction-tuned models from six vendor families:
*   Six open models, spanning a parameter range from 1.2 billion to 671 billion parameters.
*   Three deployed proprietary systems, accessed via their standard hosted interfaces.
A total of 10,664 trajectories were analyzed across the streaming and agentic task families. Decoding temperature was fixed for agentic determinism, and random seeds were recorded to ensure reproducibility. The primary metric was task success, verified against a ground-truth simulator.

**4.4 Statistical Procedure**
*   **Paired Comparisons:** Due to the reuse of task instances across context regimes, comparisons were computed as paired differences, increasing statistical power.
*   **Confidence Intervals:** Wilson score intervals were used for per-cell success rates, as these are more appropriate for proportions, especially when rates are close to 0 or 1, compared to normal approximations.
*   **Model Selection:** The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was employed to select among geometric, threshold, and linear decay shapes, favoring models that provide a better fit to the data with fewer parameters, rather than relying on arbitrary significance thresholds.

**4.5 Data and Code Availability**
All code, task generators, prompts, random seeds, the complete raw dataset (10,664 trajectories), and the analysis scripts used to reproduce all reported figures and numbers are publicly available.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding the degradation of LLM agents across varying task horizons and context conditions.

**5.1 Degradation Follows a Geometric Law**
Task success was observed to decline monotonically with increasing horizon across all models and task families where the task was non-trivial. When evaluating against geometric, threshold, and linear functional forms using AIC, the geometric form was found to best describe the decay in 28 out of 36 model-task cells. The remaining cells exhibited a threshold or cliff shape, where stronger models maintained near-perfect accuracy up to a critical horizon before failing. The study concluded that per-step reliability never reaches 1 on any non-trivial task tested, indicating that eventual collapse at a sufficiently long horizon is a mathematical certainty, not merely a possibility. The rate of decay varied across tasks: Refchain showed the flattest decay for stronger models, Ledger exhibited moderate decay, and Cipher displayed the steepest decay for all models, suggesting that task structure significantly influences the decay curve.

**5.2 Agentic Task (ToolQA) Collapse**
On the ToolQA task, which involves a genuine ReAct-style tool-use loop, a severe degradation was observed. At the shortest horizon (H=2), eight of the nine models achieved near-perfect success. However, by horizon 16, every model in the study, including widely deployed proprietary systems, had collapsed to a small fraction of its initial performance. For instance, Qwen2.5-72B, which achieved perfect success at H=2 and H=4, dropped to approximately one in eight successful trials by H=16. This rapid and universal collapse on an agentic task was identified as the most operationally significant finding.

**5.3 Per-step Reliability Rises with Scale but Never Reaches One**
Across the open-model spectrum, per-step reliability showed a positive correlation with parameter count (Pearson r=+0.36 vs. log10(params)). Proprietary models clustered with the strongest open models but did not demonstrate a wide margin of superiority. The findings suggest that model scale contributes to higher per-step reliability but with diminishing returns, and no model achieved perfect per-step reliability on any non-trivial task within this study. This inherent sub-unity reliability implies that task success will geometrically decay to near zero at a sufficiently long horizon for all models.

**5.4 Degradation Accelerates Within a Trajectory**
The study found that the hazard rate is not constant, indicating that degradation within a trajectory is not purely geometric. Pooling across long-horizon trajectories, the mean per-step accuracy decreased from 0.58 in the first third of a trajectory to 0.44 in the last third. The first error typically occurred approximately one-third of the way through a trajectory, and agents rarely recovered once an error was made. This behavior suggests that errors act as absorbing states that propagate silently through the system.

**5.5 The Driver is Step Count, Not Context Length**
The comparison across context regimes provided insight into the mechanism of degradation. The natural regime consistently performed best at every horizon. Both interventions—compressed and padded regimes—resulted in worse or similar performance compared to the natural regime. Specifically, bounding the context window in the compressed regime steepened the decay (logit slope -0.69) compared to the natural regime (-0.44), a statistically significant difference (p=3x10^-6). This outcome directly contradicts predictions based on a "lost-in-the-middle" explanation. The padded regime, where all operations were delivered in a single turn, showed a decay rate (logit slope -0.40) statistically indistinguishable from the natural regime, albeit from a lower starting success point. This finding suggests that multi-turn conversational structure itself is not the primary driver of degradation. The collective evidence points to degradation being intrinsic to executing many dependent steps rather than being primarily caused by context length. A direct practical implication is that common production tactics like naive context truncation, used to manage cost and latency, are likely to be counterproductive for reliability.

**5.6 Mechanism of Failure**
Analysis of failed trajectories revealed that "format and tool-call drift"—outputs that could not be parsed as valid actions—affected 21% of trajectories, with its incidence rising with horizon. This points to interface-level failures compounding with reasoning failures. The observed pattern of early error concentration and non-recovery after an error suggests that errors behave as absorbing states, similar to how hallucinations can persist without external verification.

**5.7 The Benchmark Gap, Quantified**
Using a mean measured per-step reliability (r=0.61) derived from the study, the geometric law was applied to project task success onto horizons representative of various benchmarks and production scenarios. The projected success rates were: 0.42 at GAIA-length horizons (8 steps), 0.36 at WebArena-length horizons (15 steps), 0.33 at Tau-Bench-length horizons (20 steps), 0.30 at SWE-bench/OSWorld-length horizons (30 steps), and 0.24 at typical hundred-step production horizons. These projections illustrate that benchmarks generally sample the flatter, higher-success portion of the curve, while the agentic task results demonstrate that a severe collapse can occur within as few as sixteen self-directed steps.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has several significant implications for the development, evaluation, and deployment of large language model (LLM) agents.

**For Practitioners and Engineering Teams:**
*   **Re-evaluation of Reliability Metrics:** The primary finding that agent task success decays geometrically with horizon implies that aggregate benchmark scores obtained at short horizons are poor predictors of reliability in long, multi-step production workflows. Teams responsible for agent orchestration and reliability should measure per-step reliability directly on their production-relevant tasks.
*   **Horizon-Aware Design and Budgeting:** The achievable operational horizon for an agent should be treated as a function of its measured per-step reliability, rather than extrapolated from short-horizon benchmark performance. Reliability budgeting should be conservative, accounting for the observed acceleration of the hazard rate within a trajectory, where errors act as absorbing states and recovery is infrequent.
*   **Caution Against Context Truncation:** The finding that degradation is driven by step count rather than context length suggests that common production shortcuts, such as naive context truncation to manage cost and latency, are likely counterproductive for agent reliability, as they steepen the decay curve.
*   **Emphasis on Step-Level Verification:** Given that errors often occur early in a trajectory and propagate silently, waiting for end-to-end task failure for detection is inefficient. Implementing step-level verification—where oracles or cheap consistency checks can be inserted—is likely to catch failures earlier and more cost-effectively.

**For Benchmark Designers:**
*   **Horizon-Resolved Reporting:** To make benchmarks more predictive of real-world performance, reporting task success resolved against the horizon, rather than as a single aggregate pass-rate, is crucial. This would provide a more complete picture of an agent's capabilities and limitations across varying task complexities.
*   **Inclusion of Agentic, Long-Horizon Tasks:** Benchmarks should incorporate genuinely agentic, tool-use tasks with significantly longer horizons to better reflect production conditions and reveal the collapse observed in this study.

**Broader Research Impact:**
*   **Mathematical Framework for Degradation:** The identification of a geometric decay law provides a quantitative framework for understanding and modeling agent degradation, moving beyond qualitative observations of failure. This can inform future research into more robust agent architectures.
*   **Challenging Assumptions on Context:** The direct contradiction of the "lost-in-the-middle" hypothesis for agentic degradation redirects research focus from solely context management to the intrinsic challenges of multi-step, dependent reasoning and action.
*   **Directions for Future Work:** The study highlights areas for further investigation, such as exploring agent resilience to early errors, developing methods for error recovery, and investigating the sensitivity of degradation to decoding parameters like temperature. The use of synthetic tasks, while valuable for control, also suggests a need for validating these findings on fully open-ended real-world agentic workflows to assess ecological validity.

In conclusion, this work provides empirical evidence for a fundamental challenge in deploying LLM agents reliably over long horizons, offering concrete findings and actionable recommendations for both practitioners and researchers in the field.
